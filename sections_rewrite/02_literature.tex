\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:rewrite-literature}

My model shares the feedback between AI development and economic growth studied by \citet{davidsonetal2026}, \citet{jonestonetti2026}, and \citet{erdiletal2025gate}: better AI raises output, expanding the resources available for further AI development. My model also allows better AI to improve research itself through RSI, a technological feedback related to the automation of research in \citet{davidsonetal2026} and \citet{jonestonetti2026}. The models differ in how saving and research expenditure are determined. Davidson et al. and Jones and Tonetti take saving and research allocation shares as given in their growth models. \citet{erdiletal2025gate} instead let a social planner choose investment and allocate compute between developing and deploying AI. I study a market equilibrium in which a household chooses consumption and saving, competitive firms choose production inputs, and a monopolistic AI developer chooses research expenditure to maximize discounted net profit.
%They let a social planner choose investment and compute allocation. I, in contrast, study a decentralized economy in which households choose saving, competitive firms choose production inputs, and a monopolistic AI developer chooses research investment to maximize discounted net profit. The resources devoted to improving AI therefore respond to private profit opportunities and compete with consumption and physical-capital accumulation.

I examine when AI can sustain faster growth rather than raise it only
temporarily. \citet{aghionjonesjones2019} and \citet{jonestonetti2026}
show how tasks that remain dependent on human labor can constrain growth
despite automation elsewhere.
%I hold the aggregate production weights fixed and characterize how substitution between labor and AI services, together with an upper bound on AI efficiency, determines the long-run regime.
In my model, RSI can improve AI efficiency without eliminating this labor
bottleneck. Conversely, when AI and labor are substitutes and AI is already
sufficiently efficient, capital accumulation and expanding compute use can
sustain permanently faster growth without further improvements in AI
efficiency. This connects to \citet{restrepo2025agi}, where output grows
with the exogenous expansion of compute once bottleneck tasks have been
automated.
%When AI and labor are substitutes, I identify an efficiency threshold above which AI-dominated equilibria exist even though AI efficiency approaches an upper bound.

Automation can raise wages while reducing labor's income share
\citep{acemoglurestrepo2018,bergbuffiezanna2018}.
\citet{bergbuffiezanna2018} show that, when robots and labor are imperfect
substitutes, higher robot productivity and capital accumulation can raise
steady-state wages while lowering labor's share. Their comparison
concerns wage levels, whereas I examine when AI can permanently increase
wage growth.
Like \citet{raymookherjee2022}, I show that wages can rise without bound
even as labor's income share vanishes. \citet{hemousolsen2022} distinguish workers by
skill: low-skill wages grow more slowly than output in the long run, while
high-skill wages keep pace with it. In my AI-dominated regime, wages grow
permanently faster than in the labor-bottleneck regime, but more slowly
than output per worker. I characterize how the elasticity of substitution
between AI and labor determines how much of the increase in
output-per-worker growth relative to the labor-bottleneck regime
translates into faster wage growth.
%I characterize these outcomes under private investment in RSI. In the AI-dominated equilibria with an upper bound on AI efficiency, wage growth permanently exceeds the exogenous rate of labor-augmenting technological progress, but output per worker grows even faster. An exact relationship links this wage-growth premium to the substitution elasticity and the rate at which labor's share declines. Separating AI revenue from compute expenditure and net profit also shows how the industry's growing share of output is distributed between its resource costs and its owners' income.

I distinguish the profits earned by the AI developer from the income
earned by supplying physical capital. This distinction connects to
\citet{korinekstiglitz2019}, who separate the gains earned by innovators
from the redistribution caused by changes in wages and other factor
prices. \citet{korinekng2019} show how digital innovation can increase
monopoly profits as a share of income while reducing the shares paid
to labor and physical capital. I examine how wages, capital income,
and developer profits evolve as private investment in RSI improves
AI efficiency and physical capital accumulates.

Changes in asset returns can also affect how the gains from automation
are distributed across households. \citet{mollrachelrestrepo2022} show
that automation can raise returns to wealth and increase income and
wealth inequality. In my model, the long-run return to physical capital
is higher in the AI-dominated regime than in the labor-bottleneck regime.
However, the representative household owns both physical capital and
the AI developer. I therefore characterize changes in the sources of
income, rather than how differences in asset ownership distribute those
gains across households.
