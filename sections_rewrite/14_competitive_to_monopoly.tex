\subsection{From competitive provision to monopoly with RSI}
\label{subsec:rewrite-competitive-transition}

I now ask whether research financed by monopoly rents can compensate for
the restriction on AI use caused by monopoly pricing and allow an economy to escape the labor bottleneck.
I set $\sigma=1.5$ and compare continued competition with monopoly under
$\chi=7.5$ and $\chi=1.5$. Unlike the preceding exercises, RSI is available
before date zero, with the same positive $\chi$ as after the change.
Research is nevertheless zero under the competitive arrangement in
Section~\ref{sec:rewrite-competition}, because improvements cannot be
privately appropriated.

I retain the technology parameters in Table~\ref{tab:rewrite-rsi-parameters},
but choose $K_0=6.535$ to place the economy on its competitive BGP at the
same $B_0$. Before the change, $p_X=1/B_0$, $r=\rho+\gamma$, and
$K_0/Y_{0^-}=3.3$. Initial efficiency lies below
$\mathcal B^{\mathrm{mc}}(1.5)$, so continued competition preserves the
labor-bottleneck BGP. The upper bound instead exceeds $\mathcal B(1.5)$,
allowing the monopoly paths considered here to approach AI-dominated growth.

At date zero, an unexpected permanent exclusive right covers both the
existing AI technology and its subsequent improvements. In particular,
competitive suppliers can no longer use the old technology. Each monopoly
path inherits the competitive $K_0$ and $B_0$ but chooses consumption and
research anew. This experiment changes research incentives and pricing,
not the RSI technology. It abstracts from the cost of enforcing exclusivity
and is not an optimal policy exercise.

Figure~\ref{fig:rewrite-competitive-sigma15-levels} compares both research
productivities with continued competition from the same initial stocks.
Panels A--C show output per worker, wages, and consumption per person.
The gray dashed line denotes continued competition; the solid blue and
dash-dotted gold lines denote monopoly with $\chi=7.5$ and $\chi=1.5$.
The upper row includes two pre-event years and years 0--10, measured as
percentages of the competitive levels. The middle and lower rows cover
years 10--100 and 10--500, measured as multiples on logarithmic scales.
The competitive benchmark is therefore $100.0\%$ or $1\times$.

The upper row shows the initial restriction in AI use before efficiency
can improve: output falls by $10.4\%$ and wages by $5.4\%$ under either
$\chi$. These are level losses at unchanged capital and efficiency, not
annual growth rates. With $\chi=7.5$, research subsequently raises
efficiency enough for output to regain its competitive counterpart around
year 3.8 and wages around year 4.6. With $\chi=1.5$, both remain below
their competitive counterparts throughout the first decade.

Consumption responds differently. It rises by $8.2\%$ at the grant under
high research productivity and by $3.7\%$ under low productivity, even
though output falls. The household chooses less capital accumulation,
while reduced inference use releases resources. Research initially
absorbs $3.3\%$ of output with $\chi=7.5$, compared with $0.6\%$ with
$\chi=1.5$. Research productivity thus changes the initial allocation,
not only the timing of recovery.

The middle row makes the slower recovery visible: output and wages under
$\chi=1.5$ regain their competitive counterparts around years 24.0 and
26.0. The lower row shows an expanding gap under both monopoly paths.
Research allows efficiency to rise beyond the monopoly threshold, whereas
continued competition retains $B_0$ and remains labor-bottlenecked.
Limiting output-per-worker growth, wage growth, and the interest rate are
$3.1\%$, $2.4\%$, and $7.1\%$ under either $\chi$, as characterized in
Proposition~\ref{prop:rewrite-equilibrium-regimes}.
The simulated horizon does not establish completed convergence: the
interest rate at year 500 is still $7.2\%$ with high research productivity
and $8.2\%$ with low research productivity.

I interpret this comparison as an institutional counterfactual, not a
calibrated prediction of granting AI exclusivity. The recovery dates
compare contemporaneous levels, not cumulative gains or household welfare.
Evaluating the policy would require the entire consumption path.
Appendix~\ref{app:rewrite-competitive-transition} retains both research
productivities for all four elasticities, including their initial stocks,
level comparisons, growth, prices, income shares, and compute expenditure.
