\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:rewrite-literature}

My model shares the feedback between AI development and economic growth studied by \citet{davidsonetal2026}, \citet{jonestonetti2026}, and \citet{erdiletal2025gate}: better AI raises output, expanding the resources available for further AI development. My model also allows better AI to improve research itself through RSI, a technological feedback related to the automation of research in \citet{davidsonetal2026} and \citet{jonestonetti2026}. The models differ in how saving and research expenditure are determined. Davidson et al. and Jones and Tonetti take saving and research allocation shares as given in their growth models. \citet{erdiletal2025gate} instead let a social planner choose investment and allocate compute between developing and deploying AI. I study a market equilibrium in which a household chooses consumption and saving, competitive firms choose production inputs, and a monopolistic AI developer chooses research expenditure to maximize discounted net profit.
%They let a social planner choose investment and compute allocation. I, in contrast, study a decentralized economy in which households choose saving, competitive firms choose production inputs, and a monopolistic AI developer chooses research investment to maximize discounted net profit. The resources devoted to improving AI therefore respond to private profit opportunities and compete with consumption and physical-capital accumulation.

I examine when AI can sustain faster growth rather than raise it only
temporarily. \citet{aghionjonesjones2019} and \citet{jonestonetti2026}
show how tasks that remain dependent on human labor can constrain growth
despite automation elsewhere.
\citet{palivoskaragiannis2010} show how sufficiently high substitutability
between capital and labor can allow capital accumulation to sustain growth
without technological progress. \citet{restrepo2025agi} obtains sustained
output growth from an exogenous expansion of compute after bottleneck tasks
have been automated.
%I hold the aggregate production weights fixed and characterize how substitution between labor and AI services, together with an upper bound on AI efficiency, determines the long-run regime.
In my model, RSI can improve AI efficiency without eliminating this labor
bottleneck. I also identify conditions under which capital and AI services
expand together and sustain permanently faster growth without further
AI-efficiency gains.
%When AI and labor are substitutes, I identify an efficiency threshold above which AI-dominated equilibria exist even though AI efficiency approaches an upper bound.

\citet{acemoglurestrepo2018} show that capital accumulation can raise
long-run wages even as automation displaces labor from existing tasks
and reduces labor's income share. Similarly, \citet{bergbuffiezanna2018}
show that, when robots and labor are imperfect substitutes, higher robot
productivity and capital accumulation can raise steady-state wages while
lowering labor's share. These comparative-static results concern wage
levels, whereas I examine when AI can permanently increase wage growth.
Like \citet{raymookherjee2022}, I show that wages can rise without bound
even as labor's income share vanishes. \citet{hemousolsen2022} show that
low-skill wages grow more slowly than output in the long run, while
high-skill wages keep pace with it. In this paper, I abstract from
labor heterogeneity.
%I characterize these outcomes under private investment in RSI. In the AI-dominated equilibria with an upper bound on AI efficiency, wage growth permanently exceeds the exogenous rate of labor-augmenting technological progress, but output per worker grows even faster. An exact relationship links this wage-growth premium to the substitution elasticity and the rate at which labor's share declines. Separating AI revenue from compute expenditure and net profit also shows how the industry's growing share of output is distributed between its resource costs and its owners' income.

I also study how market structure affects AI development and growth.
\citet{korinekng2019} examine how digital innovation lowers production
costs while generating monopoly rents and markups that restrict output.
Holding AI capability fixed, \citet{gans2025aiknowledge} shows how
competition and monopoly can affect long-run growth by changing the
choice between incremental and exploratory research.
\citet{liuwan2026} show how the prices charged by general-purpose AI
suppliers affect incentives to improve both their models and the
specialized applications that use them, shaping long-run growth.
I compare monopoly and competition to examine whether monopoly-led
research raises AI efficiency enough to compensate for the reduction
in AI use caused by monopoly pricing.

%I distinguish the AI developer's profits from income earned by supplying physical capital. \citet{korinekng2019} make this distinction in a model where digital innovation can increase monopoly profits as a share of income while reducing the shares paid to labor and physical capital. This also relates to \citet{korinekstiglitz2019}, who distinguish innovators' gains from redistribution through changes in factor prices. In my model, the long-run return to physical capital is higher in the AI-dominated regime than in the labor-bottleneck regime. This connects to \citet{mollrachelrestrepo2022}, who show how automation can raise returns to wealth and increase income and wealth inequality. My representative household owns both physical capital and the AI developer, so I examine how AI changes the composition of income, rather than how unequal asset ownership distributes its gains across households.
